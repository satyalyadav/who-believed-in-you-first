    Who believed in you first?

    Cosign went live on 25 September with a promise: a public record of who backed whom, kept by the people who did the backing. The United States has been publishing its own version of that record since 2001, and in machine-readable form since 2009. It is thinner than the pitch, and the shape of the gap is the interesting part.

    Built from … Form D filings pulled straight off EDGAR and parsed by hand. No enrichment layer, no paid database, and no language model anywhere in the data path. The model in Item 6 is a model of a mechanism, not of this data. Every figure here is recomputed from the filings by a second script that shares no code with the one that wrote this page, and the two fail loudly when they stop agreeing.

    Item 1The pitch, in their words

    Cosign is a curated professional network for the people and companies that drive the startup ecosystem, made by Andreessen Horowitz and introduced by Erik Torenberg and David Booth on 25 September 2026. MTS described the same day as "Wikipedia meets LinkedIn meets AngelList." The homepage headline is two words longer than that: Believe in someone, first.

    Build and browse profiles that showcase what makes someone exceptional, cosigned by the people who believe in them.

    cosign.co, homepage, retrieved 27 September 2026 cosign.co

    Strip the design language out and the mechanism is a signed endorsement: a named person attaches their name and standing to another person's record, the endorsement is attributed and permanent rather than a like that scrolls away, and the endorser is paid in visibility for finding people early. A second quote on the same page does the work of a spec: attributable context from people who have worked together.

    There are two layers to this and the gap between them is the rest of this page. The cosigns on a profile are public and attributed, which is the half the homepage sells. Underneath sits what the founders call an intent network, where members privately signal who you'd fund or work with and hear back when the interest is mutual. That signal is not published, by design, and it is exactly the part of a reputation system that would be worth the most to anyone reading it. MTS announcement Dealroom on the launch

    So the product is a registry of conviction, with the public half shown and the valuable half private. Who believed in whom, who was willing to say so, and who was early. The paper version of that record is already public and nobody has built anything on top of it, which is convenient for anyone building this one.

    Item 2The government already files it

    Rule 506 of Regulation D lets a US company sell securities without registering them, as much as it likes, provided it meets one of two sets of conditions. Under 506(b) it may not advertise, and it may sell to any number of accredited investors plus up to 35 others who are financially sophisticated. Under 506(c) it may advertise freely, provided every single purchaser is accredited and the company takes reasonable steps to verify it. investor.gov

    The price of either exemption is a notice on Form D, filed with the SEC no later than 15 days after the first sale. the form says so on page one That timing is the whole of Item 5 in one sentence: by the time the form is filed the money is already committed, and the form still records only how many people were in, never who they were.

    It asks for the company's officers, directors and promoters by name. It asks what industry it is in, what it is raising, how much it has sold, what the securities are, which exemption is being used, and how many people had already bought by the time it filed. It is one of the denser documents American corporate law produces and almost nobody opens. Here are five of them from the sample, unmodified, so you can see what the same page looks like when the issuer is a biotech, an insurer, a property vehicle and a fund. The rows with a warm tint are the four fields that matter for this story.

    What the form gives you

    Every issuer, its officers and directors, its industry bucket, the size of the raise, the securities, the exemption, and a date. Join it to the next filing from the same CIK and you get a timeline. This is enough to build a company directory, a sector map, a round-size index and a people index. It is genuinely useful and it took no AI.

    What it does not give you

    It does not ask who the investors were. Not one field. There is a place on the form to say how many people had already bought, and no place at all to say who any of them were. The count is in the public record. The vouching is not. That single omission is the subject of the rest of this page.

    Item 5The one field that counts backers

    Form D has a box, near the bottom, that asks for the total number of investors who had already invested in the offering by the time the filing was made. Every operating company in the sample answers it. None of them leaves it blank. The shape is not the one you would guess. A quarter report one or two backers, 83 of them exactly one, a third report eleven or more, and the largest single band is three to five. This is not a tail of small friends-and-family cheques. It is mostly small institutional rounds with a handful of buyers each, which is the part of the market nobody has ever felt the need for a directory.

    So the number of people who believed in a startup is on the public record, and who they were is not. The form will tell you the shape of a cap table at the granularity of a count, and nothing else. Cosign is building the missing half as a product, which is either an obvious gap or a business model, depending on how you squint.

    Item 6What a reputation layer inherits

    Everything above describes a record that mostly does not exist. The reasonable response from a product team is to build it, and to seed it with the people who are already credible, which is what a curated network is. The next question is what happens next, and that one you can answer without any data at all, because it is a property of the mechanism rather than of the market.

    Below is a model of the mechanism cosign describes, in the simplest form that still has the feature that matters. A set of people. Some of them can endorse. An endorsement is attributed and durable. Profiles below a threshold of endorsements are invisible in search. Reputation weights an endorsement, so being endorsed by someone well endorsed is worth more. Early endorsers are rewarded with reach, which cosign's own copy promises: make discoveries before anyone else.

    Run it for a few hundred rounds and look at the concentration. Then change the threshold, which is the number a product team argues about most, and watch that it barely matters. Then change who starts with reach, which nobody argues about at all, and watch that it decides everything.

    Item 7Receipts

    What this leaves out

    Form D covers US private placements under Rule 504, Rule 506(b) and Rule 506(c) of Regulation D, and Section 4(a)(5). All of those appear in the sample. What it misses is Regulation A, which files on Form 1-A, Regulation CF, which files on Form C, any offering made entirely outside the United States, and any offering where the notice was late or never filed, which the SEC does enforce. It does not miss foreign issuers: a Cayman or Luxembourg vehicle selling into the United States files a Form D like anyone else, and non-US issuers are in this sample, led by the Cayman Islands and Luxembourg. Co-investment vehicles and SPVs file as issuers in their own right, which is why two thirds of the corpus is funds filing under names that read like product companies.

    Two things about the names. A person is counted once per issuer, so an amendment that repeats a director has not put them on a second company. Generational suffixes are stripped before matching, so "Loeffler II" and "Loeffler" are the same person, which merges a father and a son who share a name and therefore makes the repeater count larger, not smaller. Telling a person from a legal entity is a judgement call, so the rule is written out in the build script and applied identically by the verification script. All of this makes the corpus noisier than reality rather than cleaner, which means the sparse graph in Item 4 is a ceiling and not a floor.
